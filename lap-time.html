What a tenth is made of

Every design meeting ends in the same currency: lap time. Ten kilos, ten kilowatts, five percent more downforce. Which is worth more? I wrote a lap-time simulator, put a GT3-class car on the Circuit de Barcelona-Catalunya, and priced each design choice in tenths of a second.

A quasi-steady-state simulator with generic GT3-class parameters, not a real car or team, and one calibrated number: tyre grip, fitted to the 2024 GT World Challenge pole lap. The simulator runs live in this page.

Values typical of the GT3 class, not any one car. With them and one calibrated grip level, the simulated lap is 1:39.09, the 2024 GT World Challenge pole time. Top speed comes out at 282 km/h. The grip match is set by hand, so the lap time proves nothing; what matters is how the lap changes when the car changes.

  1. The track

    Minimum-curvature racing line of Barcelona-Catalunya from the TUM racetrack database, 4.57 km, smoothed and sampled every 2 m. Curvature is all the car sees.

  2. The tyres

    Grip falls as load rises (load sensitivity), so moving load from one tyre to the other on an axle loses grip. Wider rear tyres are less sensitive. That is what makes balance matter.

  3. The corners

    At each speed, find the highest lateral acceleration both axles can hold, with downforce, its front-rear split and the load transfer set by centre-of-gravity height and roll stiffness.

  4. The straights

    Accelerate forward from the slowest corner, limited by power, rear traction and drag. Brake backwards from each corner, limited by the tyres and a 2 g brake-system cap. Combined grip follows a friction ellipse.

  5. The lap

    Speed at each point is the lowest of the three limits. Integrate distance over speed for the lap time. About 30 ms in your browser.

  6. One number calibrated

    Peak tyre friction, 1.84 at the reference load, sets the lap to the 2024 pole. Changing it by ±5% moves every price below by less than 10%.

The price list

The same changes, priced one at a time against the baseline car.

Lap time change against the baseline, Barcelona-Catalunya. Negative is faster.

Power is the cheapest tenth here

10 kW is worth 0.29 s, about as much as 20 kg. Barcelona has a 1 km main straight and several long full-throttle sections, so power pays on almost every one of them.

Downforce and drag pay in different places

+5% downforce gains 0.16 s, spread over every corner. −5% drag gains 0.12 s, almost all of it on the straights. The trace below shows where.

A kilo is cheap on a heavy car

10 kg costs 0.14 s here. For an F1 car at 800 kg the figure usually quoted is 0.3–0.4 s per 10 kg, because each kilo is a bigger share of the car.

Cumulative time gained along the lap. Steps show where each change pays: drag and power on the straights, downforce and mass through the corners.

Balance is worth more than parts

The biggest numbers on the price list cost nothing to build. They come from where the grip is, not how much there is.

The fastest car saturates both axles at once

In a corner the front tyres must carry 46% of the lateral force and the rears 54%, set by where the centre of gravity sits. The roll stiffness split decides which axle takes more of the load transfer, and the aero balance decides which gets more downforce. If one axle reaches its limit first, the grip still left on the other is wasted.

The baseline sits at the bottom of both curves. Moving roll stiffness 5% towards the front costs 0.53 s, the same as 37 kg. Moving it 5% to the rear costs 0.45 s. Aero balance is gentler, 0.07–0.11 s for 2%, but it grows with speed, so it decides the balance in the fast corners.

That is why a race weekend spends so much time on setup: an anti-roll bar click can be worth more than a season of weight saving.

What this does not capture

Good enough to rank design choices, not to predict a race.

  1. Steady state

    Each point is treated as if the car could hold it forever. No yaw transients, no suspension or tyre dynamics, no driver. Real cars lose time getting into and out of each state.

  2. A fixed line

    The racing line does not change with the car. A car with more downforce would take faster lines through some corners and a slightly different line elsewhere.

  3. Simple tyres and aero

    Linear load sensitivity, no temperature or wear, and downforce that does not change with ride height, pitch or yaw. A team's simulator replaces each of these with measured maps.

Less drag. More downforce.
