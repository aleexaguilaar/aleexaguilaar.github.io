Measuring downforce with damper potentiometers

Every race car logs the travel of its four dampers. Those signals already contain the downforce. This article explains how to get it out, builds the method step by step, and tests it on a simulated GT3 lap of Barcelona where the true answer is known, and on real Formula Student data.

I worked with damper-potentiometer data in the aerodynamics department of FSUPV Team, Formula Student at UPV. The method and code here are written from scratch for this article.

The principle

The springs carry the weight of the car and every extra load on it. Measure how far each one compresses and you know the force it carries.

What the sensor measures

A linear potentiometer is a resistive track with a sliding contact. It is fixed to the same two points as the damper, so when the damper compresses the rod slides in by exactly the same amount.

The logger feeds the track a reference voltage and reads the voltage at the wiper. That ratio is proportional to the position, so after a one-off calibration every sample is a damper length in millimetres, typically logged at 100 to 500 Hz.

Length alone gives the spring force. How fast the length changes gives the damper force. Both are needed for the wheel load.

Why the sum works

In a corner the outer wheels gain the load the inner wheels lose. Under braking the front gains what the rear loses. Weight transfer only moves load around, and anti-roll bars push one side down as much as they lift the other. So the sum of the four spring forces, relative to standstill, is the aerodynamic load.

Move the sliders: the corners change, the sum follows only the speed. The rest of the article is about where that ideal breaks.

The method, step by step

Six steps from four raw potentiometer traces to one CLA and a map of where on the lap the car makes its load.

  1. Zero at standstill

    A few seconds stationary at the start of the log gives the reference length of each damper. Everything after is measured from there, so the weight of the car and the gas force inside the dampers drop out.

  2. Filter

    A zero-phase low-pass at about 8 Hz keeps body motion and removes sensor noise and wheel hop. Zero-phase matters: a lag between force and speed biases the fit.

  3. Damper travel to wheel force

    Fi = IRi ( ki Δxi + ci(ẋi) )

    Spring rate k and damper curve c at the damper, installation ratio IR from damper to wheel by virtual work. The damper term only matters in transients and over bumps.

  4. Sum the four corners

    S = Σ Fi

    Lateral transfer, longitudinal transfer and anti-roll bar forces cancel. What remains is aero, plus two effects that do not cancel.

  5. Remove what is not aero

    DF = S + Fjack − Fanti

    Jacking: tyre side forces acting through roll centres above the ground lift the body. Anti-dive and anti-squat: part of the braking and traction transfer goes through the links, not the springs. Both come from the suspension geometry and the measured accelerations.

  6. Fit, don't average

    DF = ½ ρ CLA v²

    One least-squares fit of DF against dynamic pressure, per lap or per section, with a block bootstrap for the confidence interval. Averaging DF/q point by point blows up at low speed.

A lap where the answer is known

Real data never tells you the true CLA. So I simulated one: a GT3-class car with CLA 2.80, a quarter-car model at each corner with digressive dampers, unsprung mass, tyre stiffness and road roughness, driven at the limit around the real centreline of Barcelona. Lap time 1:43.8, top speed 282 km/h.

What the corners hide

On the straights the plain sum is already right: 2.82 against 2.80. In the corners it reads 0.8 to 2.1, because jacking lifts the car by an amount that grows with lateral g. Over the whole lap that drags the answer down to 2.49.

With the jacking and anti-geometry terms removed, every section lands between 2.82 and 2.86, and the full lap gives 2.83.

What has to be right

Change one input at a time and see how far CLA moves. Spring rate and installation ratio pass straight through: a 5% error is a 4.5% error. A 0.2 mm error in the static reference is worth 3%.

The damper curve barely matters over a lap. Roll-centre heights do, but only through the corner correction: even 30% wrong, the corrected lap stays within 4%, and the straights are unaffected.

Real data, with and without wings

Formula uOttawa publishes logs from their GGR26 under an MIT licence. On 3 August 2026 they ran the same layout all afternoon with aero, then again without it: the cleanest test of the method a team can run.

The wings show up

Front axle compression on straights, binned by speed. With wings the front sinks 4.3 mm per (100 km/h)² (95% interval 3.7 to 4.8). Without wings it stays flat or rises slightly, −1.6 mm.

The team does not publish spring rates, so this stays in millimetres. The rear-right sensor was saturated in the no-aero runs, so only the front axle compares fairly.

And so does the jacking

In left-hand corners at 1.3 g the inner front wheel extends 9 mm while the outer one compresses only 4 mm. If weight transfer were all that happened, the two would match and the sum would stay at zero. It drops to −5 mm instead: the same signature as the simulation, and the reason the corner correction exists.

Running it on a real car

The code is here in Python, about a hundred lines. What makes the result trustworthy is how the test is run.

Calibrate in the garage

Put a known weight on each corner and check sign, installation ratio and spring rate against the potentiometer reading. Most errors live here.

Standstill in every log

A few seconds stationary at the start and end, so sensor drift and fuel burn can be removed from the reference.

Straights for CLA

Steady runs in both directions to cancel wind and slope give the cleanest number. Corners are for balance and the aero map.

Know the geometry

Roll-centre heights and anti percentages from the kinematics model turn the corners from noise into data.

Map by section

One fit per section shows where the car makes and loses load: low-speed corners, braking zones, kerbs.

Aero on and off

The same layout with and without the wings is the strongest validation of the whole chain.
