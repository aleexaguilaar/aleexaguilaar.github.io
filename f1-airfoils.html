Why F1 teams draw their own airfoils

You will not find a NACA section on a Formula 1 wing. Catalogue airfoils were designed for aircraft, drones or gliders, each for a different job. An F1 wing runs upside down, near the ground, inside a box set by the rules, and has to make as much downforce as possible for the drag it costs. So I designed a section for that job and compared it with the classics. Then I put it near the ground.

Single-element sections in 2D, analysed with NeuralFoil and checked with XFOIL at Re = 106, plus my own panel method for ground effect. Real F1 wings are multi-element and three-dimensional, so the numbers show why custom sections win, not what a team's wing makes.

What a catalogue airfoil is for

Every famous airfoil was designed to win at someone else's problem.

Drawn upside down, as they would sit on a car: suction side towards the ground. Tap a name to show or hide it.

  • NACA 44121930s, for aircraft wings. Good lift-to-drag in cruise, gentle stall. Max CL 1.63 here.
  • LS(1)-0413NASA, 1970s, for light aircraft. Tyrrell started its 1998 front wing from it.
  • Selig S12231990s, for maximum lift at low Reynolds number, around 2 × 105: slow, heavy-lift drones. Max CL 2.40 here.

What an F1 section needs instead

Downforce, not lift, so it runs inverted. A lift coefficient above 2 per element. Reynolds numbers around 106. On the front wing, a ground that changes the pressure under it. Elements that work together through slot gaps. Shapes that fit the boxes in the rules and pass the FIA deflection tests. Enough stall margin to survive yaw, ride-height changes and dirty air. And a section that changes along the span.

Designing one

Write the job down as an optimisation problem and let the shape follow.

  1. Shape

    Kulfan (CST) parameterisation: 8 weights per side plus a leading-edge term, 0.3% chord trailing edge. Seventeen numbers describe the whole section.

  2. Physics

    NeuralFoil, a physics-informed neural network trained on XFOIL, gives CL and CD in milliseconds and is differentiable, so a gradient optimiser can use it.

  3. The job

    Maximise CL at Re = 106 for a given drag budget. Lift must still be rising 3° later (stall margin), and the model must be confident at both points.

  4. The rules of thumb

    At least 9% thick at 30% chord and 1.5% at 85% for structure, 3.5% at 3% chord for a robust nose. No other shape restriction.

  5. Six drag budgets

    CD from 0.010 to 0.025, one optimised section each. Together they trace the best downforce each drag level can buy.

  6. Independent check

    Every section rerun in XFOIL with the same stall-margin rule. XFOIL agrees within 3% on CL and gives 5–14% more drag than NeuralFoil.

XFOIL, Re = 106, Ncrit = 9. Custom line: the section optimised for CD ≤ 0.015. Diamonds: all six optimised sections at their design points. Positive incidence only in the drag view.

+40% downforce, same drag

At CD ≈ 0.015 the custom section gives CL = 2.40 with 3° of stall margin left. The Selig S1223, the best catalogue high-lift section, gives 1.71 under the same rule, and the NACA 4412 gives 1.34.

It looks like the job

Much more camber towards the back, a thick, round nose that tolerates incidence changes, and a thick body. It is 15% thick: the optimiser was free to go thinner and did not.

Nothing is free

The pitching moment grows: CM −0.36 against −0.10 for the NACA 4412. That load twists the wing and its mounts, and F1 wings have to pass deflection tests. A real design would put a limit on it.

Then add the ground

A front wing works a few centimetres from the track. That changes the pressure under it, so a section that is best in free air is not best there.

Inviscid panel method with an image wing, 4° incidence, clearance from the lowest point. Inviscid flow never separates, so the gains near the ground are too large; real wings stall below about 10% chord.

The ground pulls harder on the suction side

I wrote a panel method (Hess–Smith, checked against XFOIL inviscid within 2–3%) and modelled the ground with a mirror-image wing. The gap between wing and track acts like a venturi. At 10% chord the custom section's suction peak grows from Cp −2.7 to −24, and the inviscid downforce rises about five times.

Real air does not follow. Zerihan and Zhang tested a wing in a Southampton tunnel with a moving ground. Downforce rose strongly below 20% chord and dropped below about 10%, when the boundary layer separated at the trailing edge. The bigger the suction peak, the steeper the climb back to free-stream pressure behind it, and the sooner the flow separates.

That wing was the main element of the 1998 Tyrrell 026 front wing. Tyrrell had started from the NASA LS(1)-0413, then reduced the suction-side camber, moved its lowest point forward, flattened the pressure side and reshaped the leading edge. That is a section redesigned for the ground, in the 1990s.

The rule-book airfoil

There was one standard airfoil in F1, and the FIA drew it.

2009–2013: a mandated centre section

The central 500 mm of the front wing had to follow a profile defined point by point in the technical regulations, with a tolerance of ±1 mm (±2 mm at the trailing edge). Teams were free everywhere else on the wing.

2022 on: boxes, not shapes

The rules limit the front wing to four elements inside reference volumes, with rules on curvature and planform. Inside those boxes the sections are each team's own, and they vary across the span to steer the flow.

2026: two shapes for one wing

With active aero the front and rear wing flaps move between a high-downforce corner mode and a low-drag straight mode. Each section has to work in both, which I studied in my BSc thesis on 2026 wings.

And the Gurney flap

In 1971 Dan Gurney fitted a small right-angle strip to his car's rear wing. Bob Liebeck, a Douglas aerodynamicist who designed high-lift sections from target pressure distributions, tested it and gave it its name. At 1–2% of chord it adds lift for a small drag cost. It is the clearest example of racing shaping a section for itself.

What this does not capture

Enough to show why teams design their own sections, not enough to design a team's wing.

  1. One element, 2D

    F1 wings have up to four elements with slots, plus endplates, tip vortices and spanwise twist. The slot flow changes each element's best shape.

  2. Models near stall

    NeuralFoil and XFOIL are least reliable close to maximum lift, where the optimiser pushes. The 3° stall margin and the XFOIL recheck limit, but do not remove, that risk.

  3. Inviscid ground effect

    The panel method shows how the pressure field changes near the ground but cannot predict separation. A ground-effect design needs a viscous solver, RANS or a coupled boundary layer, with a moving ground.

Less drag. More downforce.
